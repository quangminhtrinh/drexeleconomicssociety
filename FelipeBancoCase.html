Financial Fraud and Institutional Autonomy in Brazil: Lessons from the Banco Master Collapse

  • May 10, 2025

  • Felipe Correa Gomes

In the dusk of 2025, Brazil’s banking system was shaken by the collapse of Banco Master, a mid-sized but extremely fast-growing bank, whose liquidation exposed deep-seated issues in risk management, regulatory supervision, and the role of the largest South American central bank. What began as an aggressive growth investment strategy, fueled by unusually high-yield portfolios, ultimately revealed a complex web of alleged fraud, structural weakness, and political influence. This left investors, regulators, and the public questioning the strength of Brazilian financial oversight and the importance of independent institutions. This case, thus, offers a great example of why the central bank must act autonomously and proactively in order to prevent systemic instability.

Banco Master built a big brand in the Latin country by offering certificates of bank deposits (CDBs), a fixed-income investment, at interest rates significantly above market averages, sometimes reporting up to 140% of the CDI benchmark (which closely follows the SELIC Central Bank rate). Those promised returns attracted widespread attention and a fast-growing base of depositors. However, the high rates raised concerns among analysts and regulators about the sustainability of the bank’s assets, as high yields are connected to higher risks in financial markets; it seemed highly odd for “risk-free” bank deposits. There was also a big worry about the increasing rates in Brazil compressing the bank’s cash flow capability to service its debt.

Following the rapid growth of deposits and money inflow, the Master’s board led an expansion of the bank's assets into complex and illiquid investments, aiming to generate high-yield returns. Despite reports that assets totaled tens of billions of reais, at the end of 2025, an assessment revealed that the bank only had a few million reais in cash, exposing a big sign of liquidity stress and growing disconnection between published and real figures.

Therefore, last November, Brazil’s central bank (Bacen) coordinated an extrajudiciary liquidation of Banco Master, effectively shutting down the bank's operations and placing it under a court-appointed administrator to manage the closure and payments to creditors. The liquidation followed mounting evidence of irregularities and potential financial fraud. Federal investigations indicate that the bank had created and marketed fraudulent and fictitious credit portfolios and inaccurately valued assets in order to present a stronger and inflated balance sheet. Some of the credit portfolios sold by the bank are likely to have never even existed or were significantly overvalued. In practice, those maneuvers mean that the bank recorded expected future payments from borrowers and investments that were not supported by strong or real contracts and guarantees.

Additionally, authorities found evidence that some structured credit operations were used to move assets between different capital market entities in ways that would omit the bank’s risk. Some of these portfolios were transferred or pledged in transactions involving BRB, a state-controlled bank that had considered buying Master before the deal got blocked by regulators. Those transactions are likely to have created false liquidity and solvency for Master’s operations while the bank was facing increasing stress.

Shortly after the liquidation order, under the “Compliance Zero” investigation, Brazilian authorities arrested Daniel Vorcaro, the president of Banco Master, as part of a broader web of fraudulent management practices. Some prosecutors describe the case as one of the largest banking fraud cases in Brazilian history.

Although Brazil’s Credit Guarantee Fund (FGC), similar to the American FDIC, protected thousands of investors that had deposits under the Fund’s cap (R$250,000 or approximately USD 49,000), limiting immediate contagion, the episode raised deeper questions about supervision timing and enforcement. The central bank ultimately intervenes, but the scale of alleged irregularities suggests that warning signs might have been ignored and not translated into corrective action. The high-yield deposit products funded by questionable credit structures and investments created a particularly dangerous dynamic where the accelerated growth occurred due to a risk underestimation.

This is where the idea of central bank independence becomes central. Recent investigation reports indicate that Mr. Vorcaro maintains a high-level political network and connections, being able to meet influential figures and policymakers in Brasília during the Master’s growth and stress phases. While political access does not imply wrongdoing, it raises legitimate concerns about regulatory forbearance and potential softening of supervisory pressure.
